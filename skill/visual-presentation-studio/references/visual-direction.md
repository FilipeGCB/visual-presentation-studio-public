# Visual Direction

Visual direction is a narrative decision expressed through composition, typography, color, image language, rhythm, and motion.

## Existing identity

Treat supplied brand rules, logos, typography, colors, accessibility constraints, and prior approved materials as constraints. Distinguish mandatory identity from historical habits that may be improved.

## No supplied identity

Develop 2–3 conceptually distinct directions and recommend one. Different colors applied to the same layout are one direction, not several.

This comparison remains mandatory under deadline or authority pressure. A request to “bring no alternatives” does not create an identity and does not waive the comparison; keep it concise when time is limited.

Describe each direction with:

- concept and relationship to the thesis;
- emotional register and audience fit;
- compositional logic and information density;
- typography and color roles;
- imagery, diagram, chart, icon, and motion language;
- strengths, risks, and accessibility implications;
- representative references and what is being learned from each.

## Research discipline

Research only after the narrative need is known. Live reference research is mandatory when the user names references, asks for a premium/current/best-in-class result, or the proposed direction depends on motion, sticky behavior, parallax, or scrollytelling. Inspect 3–5 relevant examples unless fewer defensible sources exist. It may be omitted only when a supplied identity already fixes the direction and no reference-dependent behavior is proposed; record the reason.

Useful sources may include 21st.dev, Awwwards, editorial design, production sites, component libraries, motion libraries, data visualization work, icon systems, photography, diagrams, maps, and relevant digital experiences. Treat portfolio galleries such as Dribbble as visual studies and verify production feasibility elsewhere when behavior matters.

References supply principles, not a finished identity. Never copy proprietary text, images, brand language, or a near-identical exclusive layout. Record the transferable principle: hierarchy, pacing, spatial model, transition logic, data treatment, or asset behavior.

Use a reference matrix with:

- live URL, source, and creator when known;
- observed resting composition and scroll/interaction behavior;
- transferable principle and the narrative problem it solves;
- what must not be copied;
- fit with the proposed stack, accessibility, and performance constraints;
- adoption, adaptation, or rejection decision.

When interaction matters, inspect the rendered page rather than relying on thumbnails or prose descriptions. Compare the implemented result against the approved matrix during Visual Inspection.

## Recommendation test

Recommend the direction that best clarifies the thesis for the actual audience and destination. Do not choose based on novelty alone.

Ask:

1. Does the hierarchy make the intended message unavoidable?
2. Does the direction create a recognizable world for this story?
3. Is any element present only to signal “premium” or “AI-made sophistication”?

If the third answer is yes, remove or justify the element before Gate 1.
